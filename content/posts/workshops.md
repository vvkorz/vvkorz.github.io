# Leadership Workshops

Leadership workshop of a large corporate organisation is in full swing. More than 30 participants are actively engaged in discussions. 

Topic at hand: There are a lot of projects and activities and an obvious lack of focus. "Shall we introduce 100 $ to everyone who closes a project?" - someone proposes. Excitement raises in the room, there is a suggestion to raise the bar and introduce a bounty program to hunt for projects and kill them. Another person suggests a more methodological approach and proposes to define killer criteria at the beginning of every project. Discussion erupts, are killer criteria those that define how project should be killed or those that define killer features that make the project successful? Tension ... You open X: SpaceX catched 52.1 meter tall and 9 meter wide Starship successfully...

"We are just really bad at stopping projects!" - someone exclaims. After 30 minutes of discussion comes an action item: *Every project needs to have a definition of done!* Everyone nods... Who is the owner? one proactive person raises a hand and suggest to form a working group that in one month will deliver definition of done for projects. Everyone nods... Let's call it DoD for short. Everyone nods... You look at top hackernews post that is about openai solving another math problem..."

"There is a lack of dedication in staffing the projects, people are too spread across multiple activities and have a lot of context switch overhead." - someone points out. The room nods in agreement. New rule is born: no more than 2 projects per person at any given time. No activity less than 3 weeks of full work will be accepted as a valid project. You open Discord and read latest reviews about the new rich world of GTA VI. 

Workshop is over. Newly inspired leaders return to their teams with a bunch of new rules how to work: 
- Every project must have a clear definition of done (DoD), that follows agreed-upon criteria.
- No more than 2 projects per person at any given time.
- No project should be shorter than 3 weeks of full work.
- etc ...

I looked out of the window and thought for a second: "What exactly makes me fee so bad?". Is it the moderation? participants? lack of air?
Why making things simple, if you can make them complex? Why talk about results and achievements if you can talk about processes, methodologies, and rules? Why is it happening, that a bunch of people with PhDs in STEM sciences spends so much of their lifetime discussing trivial matters? 

And then it hit me: Lack of pressure. A collegue once said: "If we are not honest to ourselves, market will make us honnest." And I think this is it. At the absence of pressure for results, curious minds start to invent things. Engineers will always engineer something, scientists will always find the way to discuss hypotheses and test them. 

It is easy to criticise, but can I come up wit ha better framework for a workshop like so? Perhaps, instead of focus on methodologies and *amount* of projects, each team should just present top 2-3 projects and results achieved. The generated impact should be discussed with physicological safety and brutal candor. If there was impact, talk about methods and processes that led to it result, may be others will learn from it. If there was no impact, again talk about methods and proceses that led ot it, others will learn. Move on. This world is pretty random and what might work today wont work tomorrow, constantly adapt and dont bother setting processes in stone for a lifetime, focus on generated impact, celebrate it and move on to building new things.
