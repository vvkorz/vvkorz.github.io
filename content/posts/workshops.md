# Leadership Workshops

The leadership workshop of a large corporate organisation is in full swing. More than 30 participants are actively engaged in discussions. 

The topic at hand: there are a lot of projects and activities and an obvious lack of focus. "Shall we give $100 to everyone who closes a project?" someone proposes. Excitement rises in the room; there is a suggestion to raise the bar and introduce a bounty program to hunt down projects and kill them. Another person suggests a more methodical approach and proposes defining killer criteria at the beginning of every project. A discussion erupts: are killer criteria those that define how a project should be killed, or those that define the killer features that make the project successful? Tension... You open X: SpaceX has successfully caught the 52.1-meter-tall, 9-meter-wide Starship...

"We are just really bad at stopping projects!" someone exclaims. After 30 minutes of discussion comes an action item: *Every project needs to have a definition of done!* Everyone nods... Who is the owner? One proactive person raises a hand and suggests forming a working group that will deliver a definition of done for projects within a month. Everyone nods... Let's call it DoD for short. Everyone nods... You look at the top Hacker News post, which is about OpenAI solving another math problem...

"There is a lack of dedication in staffing the projects; people are spread too thin across multiple activities and have a lot of context-switching overhead," someone points out. The room nods in agreement. A new rule is born: no more than 2 projects per person at any given time. No activity shorter than 3 weeks of full-time work will be accepted as a valid project. You open Discord and read the latest reviews of the rich new world of GTA VI.

The workshop is over. Newly inspired leaders return to their teams with a bunch of new rules on how to work:
- Every project must have a clear definition of done (DoD) that follows agreed-upon criteria.
- No more than 2 projects per person at any given time.
- No project should be shorter than 3 weeks of full-time work.
- etc.

I look out of the window and think for a second: "What exactly makes me feel so bad?" Is it the moderation? The participants? The lack of air?
Why make things simple if you can make them complex? Why talk about results and achievements if you can talk about processes, methodologies, and rules? Why does it happen that a bunch of people with PhDs in STEM fields spend so much of their lives discussing trivial matters?

And then it hits me: a lack of pressure. A colleague once said: "If we are not honest with ourselves, the market will make us honest." And I think this is it. In the absence of pressure for results, curious minds start to invent things. Engineers will always engineer something; scientists will always find a way to discuss hypotheses and test them.

It is easy to criticise, but can I come up with a better framework for a workshop like this? Perhaps, instead of focusing on methodologies and the *number* of projects, each team should just present its top 2-3 projects and the results achieved. The generated impact should be discussed with psychological safety and brutal candor. If there was impact, talk about the methods and processes that led to it; maybe others will learn from it. If there was no impact, again talk about the methods and processes that led to that; others will learn. Move on. This world is pretty random, and what works today might not work tomorrow. Constantly adapt and don't bother setting processes in stone for a lifetime. Focus on generated impact, celebrate it, and move on to building new things.
