# The fallacy of national cliches

It happens in almost every international workshop I attend. Somewhere between the morning coffee and the first breakout session, someone drops a familiar cultural cliché with a knowing smile: "Well, you know how Italians are with schedules," "Germans are always punctual," or "Of course the French wants wine at dinner."

Everyone chuckles and nods. Meanwhile, I feel an immediate spike of frustration.

It isn’t just that these remarks feel lazy or mildly awkward. It’s that in my actual working life, they actively sabotage collaboration. Walking into a room armed with national stereotypes doesn't make you "culturally aware"—it puts you on the wrong foot with the real human beings sitting across the table. You stop listening to who they actually are and start filtering every word through a caricature.

For a long time, this was just a gut reaction based on lived experience. But if you look at the problem through the lens of statistics and cross-cultural research, that gut reaction turns out to be completely right. Bringing national clichés into a workshop isn't helpful shorthand—it’s just bad inference.

## Your Sample Is Tiny, and the Variance Is Huge

Even if a national average were completely accurate, applying it to the three or four people at your workshop table ignores basic variance. A large meta-analysis of 558 cultural studies (Taras et al., 2016) found that roughly 80% of variation in cultural values happens within countries, not between them. Another massive study analyzing over 168,000 group comparisons found a median overlap of 95% between populations (Hanel et al., 2019).

Think of two overlapping bell curves sitting almost directly on top of each other. Knowing which curve a person was drawn from barely narrows your guess—especially when your sample size is a handful of people in a conference room.

## You Are Conditioning on the Wrong Variable

The colleagues in your workshop are not a random draw from a national census. They have been heavily filtered by education, industry, profession, company culture, and international experience.

Research consistently shows that political borders are a weak boundary for culture compared to socioeconomic and professional groups. Two engineers—one from Milan, one from Munich—will usually have far more in common in how they communicate, plan, and solve problems than either has with the statistical average of their home country. Treating them as random draws from a national distribution ignores massive selection bias sitting right in front of you.

## The Prior Is Usually Wrong Anyway

In Bayesian thinking, leaning on a prior only helps if that prior reflects reality. National clichés rarely do. In a landmark Science study spanning 49 cultures, Terracciano et al. (2005) compared widely held stereotypes about "national character" against actual measured personality profiles. People strongly agreed on what the stereotypes were—yet those beliefs had virtually zero correlation with reality (a median correlation of just 0.04).

We aren't even anchoring on noisy population means. Most of the time, we are anchoring on hollywood stereotypes.

## The Cost of Being Wrong Is Asymmetric

Finally, human collaboration doesn't follow a symmetric error function. Even if a stereotype gave you a tiny statistical edge, being wrong because you leaned on a cliché carries a steep penalty: alienation, defensiveness, and confirmation bias that takes weeks to undo.

Starting with a flat, uninformative prior—and updating rapidly based on the actual behavior in the room—costs almost nothing and avoids the trap altogether.

Cultural differences are real, and broader cultural frameworks can be genuinely useful as diagnostic tools after friction shows up. But using national averages as an upfront shortcut for the individuals in a workshop is both socially clumsy and mathematically broken. Drop the passport priors, observe the actual sample, and let the people in front of you show you who they are.
